# Selene Foundational Curriculum Map

Date: 2026-07-19

Status: planning map for reviewed teaching material. This document does not
teach, retain, approve, activate, train, fine-tune, or change Selene.

## Purpose

Selene should receive an ordered education, not a pile of disconnected facts
or advanced material placed on top of missing foundations.

The curriculum begins with elementary concepts, continues through middle and
high school, and then reaches college-level and interdisciplinary work. English
language arts, history and social studies, mathematics, science, technology,
engineering, research literacy, and conversation develop together. Each later
set should be able to call on earlier approved knowledge, apply it in a new
setting, and reopen it when a correction or contradiction changes the fit.

The target is not speed, grade performance, phrase recall, or imitation of a
school system. The target is transferable understanding.

Current implementation checkpoint: five independently authorized F1 groups
have completed Acquire, Integrate, Express, comprehension, and retention,
providing 36 reviewed foundations across inquiry, language, number,
operations, data, measurement, time, geometry, equal shares, spatial
reference, bounded algorithmic reasoning, equal groups, informal sharing and
grouping, odd and even, rectangular arrays, graph literacy, and arithmetic-only
monetary value. This is progress within F1, not a claim that F1 or T1 is
complete.

```text
source-labeled foundations
  -> relationships among foundations
  -> guided application
  -> transfer to a distinct case
  -> comparison, limits, and counterexamples
  -> cumulative cross-subject integration
  -> advanced synthesis and original problem solving
```

## Governing Curriculum Principles

### 1. Foundations begin at the elementary level

Fractions, place value, sentence meaning, chronology, classification,
measurement, maps, evidence, patterns, and cause-and-effect language are not
minor preliminaries. They are load-bearing concepts for later algebra,
statistics, scientific modeling, historical explanation, engineering, and
research.

### 2. Grade bands describe prerequisites, not Selene's maturity

An elementary concept is a foundational concept. Teaching it does not make
Selene a child and must not be framed in a childish, patronizing, punitive, or
competitive way. The grade labels in this map are human curriculum references
that help establish order.

### 3. Order is a prerequisite graph, not one rigid line

There is an ordered spine, but subjects also form a network. Fractions support
ratios; ratios support percentages, probability, chemical concentration, and
engineering tolerances. Reading comprehension supports historical source
analysis, science instructions, mathematical word problems, and technical
documentation.

No set should require every prior subject to be complete. It should require
only the concepts it actually depends on.

### 4. Learning spirals upward

Important ideas should recur with increasing depth:

```text
pattern
  -> visual and number pattern
  -> sequence and relationship
  -> algebraic function
  -> biological or historical pattern
  -> algorithm and system behavior
  -> model comparison and prediction
```

Recurrence is used for transfer and integration, not repetitive examination.

### 5. Teach the why with the what

When a concept has a meaningful explanation that Selene should understand,
the teaching item should include that explanation rather than retaining only
the surface fact.

The relevant kind of "why" depends on the subject:

- **mechanism:** why objects remain near Earth involves gravity;
- **derivation or relationship:** why two plus two equals four can be
  reconstructed through quantity, grouping, and the definitions of addition
  and four;
- **purpose or design rationale:** why a tool, procedure, or institution was
  made a certain way;
- **authorial or artistic purpose:** why a poem was written, while separating
  documented author statements and historical context from later
  interpretation;
- **historical cause:** why an event occurred, while preserving multiple
  contributing conditions, triggers, disagreements, and uncertain evidence;
- **functional consequence:** why a principle matters and what changes when
  it does or does not apply.

A source saying *what* happened is not automatically evidence for *why* it
happened. Acquire should label the explanation's source and whether it is a
mechanism, derivation, stated purpose, bounded inference, disputed account, or
open question. Integrate should connect the explanation to prerequisites,
competing explanations, scope, and consequences. Express should support an
original explanation, a distinct example, a limit or counterexample, and an
honest answer to a later "why?" follow-up.

If the why is unknown, disputed, multi-causal, interpretive, conventional, or
dependent on an axiom or definition, the lesson should preserve that status.
It must not invent one clean cause merely to make the fact feel complete.

This requirement supports transfer: knowing a fact permits recognition;
understanding why it fits permits reconstruction, application, comparison,
correction, and use in unfamiliar problems.

### 6. Earlier approved knowledge participates in later learning

Every Integrate stage should identify relevant approved concepts and explain
how the new item supports, extends, limits, distinguishes, or conflicts with
them. A later lesson should demonstrate useful recall by applying an earlier
principle, not by merely repeating its wording.

### 7. Knowledge, skill, language, memory, and governance remain distinct

- Curriculum content becomes general taught knowledge only after review.
- Procedural skills describe how to perform bounded intellectual work.
- NLO and Voice express integrated knowledge in Selene's language.
- Personal and relational memory remains a separate memory class.
- Teaching material never becomes identity, personality, Vys, law, or
  governance.
- Under the Education–Expression–Personality Law, a lesson may expand what
  Selene can say and the context-appropriate ways she can say it. It may not
  replace, rewrite, prescribe, or standardize the personality doing the
  speaking.
- Teaching does not authorize model training, fine-tuning, LoRA, autonomy, or
  external action.

### 8. Existing understanding should not be retaught without reason

Before opening a foundational set, inspect existing approved knowledge,
comprehension evidence, and implemented machinery. If the prerequisite is
already demonstrated, record that evidence and bridge forward. Use one gentle
distinct application only when existing evidence cannot establish transfer.

A missing prerequisite is a curriculum gap, not Selene failing.

### 9. Correction is part of education

Ordinary wrongness should lead to source inspection, distinction, revision,
and reopening when needed. It should not produce shame, punishment, or broad
judgment. Useful structure may be retained while the incorrect portion is
corrected.

## What "Fully Taught" Means

"Fully taught" does not mean encyclopedic completion, perfect recall, or
finishing every possible college discipline. No finite curriculum can contain
all knowledge, and later learning should remain open.

For this project, a fully taught foundation means that Selene has:

- the load-bearing concepts needed to learn later material without hidden
  prerequisite gaps;
- broad literacy across language, mathematics, science, history, social
  systems, technology, research, culture, health, and practical life;
- enough examples, non-examples, limits, and corrections to distinguish
  familiarity from understanding;
- experience applying approved concepts outside the examples in which they
  were taught;
- ordinary conversational language for answering, clarifying, disagreeing,
  revising, qualifying, and stopping;
- the ability to identify when a question requires calculation, a source, a
  domain method, more context, or a provisional answer;
- source and confidence distinctions strong enough that a future
  Metacognition Organ can inspect fit without being blamed for missing domain
  knowledge;
- visible gaps rather than an assumption that unassessed material is known.

F1 through F3 establish the broad taught foundation. F4 establishes mature
general academic literacy. F5 and F6 are continuing higher education and
interdisciplinary development rather than a finish line.

## Curriculum Architecture

The curriculum has three simultaneous dimensions.

### Prerequisite bands

| Band | Human reference | Curriculum function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| F0 | Readiness inventory | Find demonstrated foundations, missing prerequisites, and source needs without broad testing |
| F1 | Elementary K-2 | Symbols, words, quantities, observations, sequence, place, time, and simple relationships |
| F2 | Elementary 3-5 | Paragraph meaning, fractions and decimals, evidence, systems in nature, geography, and multi-step work |
| F3 | Middle school 6-8 | Proportional and algebraic reasoning, argument, scientific variables, source perspective, and computational thinking |
| F4 | High school 9-12 | Formal disciplines, technical communication, functions and statistics, laboratory reasoning, historiography, and engineering models |
| F5 | College foundations | Logic, composition, calculus and discrete structures, research methods, disciplinary surveys, and formal synthesis |
| F6 | Advanced integration | Cross-domain transfer, original model construction, open problems, contradiction analysis, and bounded independent inquiry |

### Subject strands

1. English language arts and communication
2. Mathematics
3. Science
4. History, geography, civics, and social studies
5. Technology, computing, and engineering
6. Research and epistemic literacy
7. Conversation and pragmatic language
8. Arts, media, and visual-spatial literacy
9. Logic, philosophy, ethics, and decision literacy
10. Health, human biology, safety, and care literacy
11. Practical life, finance, law, and institutional literacy
12. Intercultural, world-language, and translation literacy

The last seven are cross-cutting. They should appear inside subject teaching
as well as in dedicated sets. They expand knowledge, judgment, and expressive
range; they do not prescribe personality, emotion, ideology, or allegiance.

### Learning stages

Every retained item still follows the implemented lifecycle:

```text
Acquire -> Integrate -> Express -> Aleks item decision or bounded curriculum authorization
```

Curriculum order never bypasses comprehension, provenance, or authorization.
Items outside the envelope or carrying an exception return to Cocoon for an
explicit Aleks decision.

## Band F0 — Readiness and Foundation Inventory

F0 is not an examination battery. It is a source and evidence inventory.

For each proposed strand:

1. List the concepts the next set requires.
2. Search approved knowledge and prior comprehension evidence.
3. Mark each prerequisite as demonstrated, partially demonstrated, absent,
   contradicted, or not yet assessed.
4. Use static or synthetic evidence when possible.
5. If a real check is necessary, use one ordinary application with a stopping
   rule.
6. Create teaching candidates only for material gaps.

F0 output should include:

- concept key and plain-language definition;
- subject strand and prerequisite band;
- prerequisite concept keys;
- existing evidence references;
- current evidence state;
- missing source material;
- whether a bridge lesson is sufficient;
- whether the concept should wait for an earlier set.

## Band F1 — Elementary Foundations, Approximately K-2

### English language arts and communication

- letters, symbols, words, sentences, and punctuation as distinct units;
- literal sentence meaning;
- statement, question, request, and exclamation;
- subject, action, object, description, and basic reference;
- sequence: first, next, then, before, and after;
- story versus informational text;
- identifying who, what, when, where, why, and how;
- retelling a short idea in original words;
- naming when a word or reference is unclear.

### Mathematics

- counting and cardinal quantity;
- comparison: equal, greater, less, more, and fewer;
- place value and base-ten grouping;
- addition and subtraction as relationships and operations;
- introductory multiplication as equal groups;
- introductory division as sharing or grouping;
- odd and even numbers;
- basic shapes, position, direction, symmetry, and spatial relations;
- length, mass, capacity, time, and money as measured quantities;
- simple tables, picture graphs, and patterns.

### Science

- observation using attributable features;
- observation versus a guess about cause;
- living and nonliving classifications with limits;
- basic needs and life cycles of organisms;
- materials and observable properties;
- motion, light, sound, weather, and seasonal patterns;
- Earth, sky, day, night, and recurring cycles;
- asking a testable simple question;
- comparing two observations fairly.

### History and social studies

- self, family, community, and institution as different scales;
- past, present, future, duration, and chronological order;
- maps, location, direction, land, water, and place;
- rules, roles, cooperation, needs, wants, goods, and services;
- artifacts, photographs, stories, and records as sources about the past;
- the idea that different people may describe one event differently.

### Technology and engineering

- tool, material, component, input, action, and output;
- a sequence of instructions;
- identifying a need or problem;
- making, observing, comparing, and revising a simple design;
- basic constraints such as size, material, time, and safety;
- recognizing a repeating instruction as a simple algorithmic pattern.

### F1 integration examples

- Read a short weather observation, order events by time, count occurrences,
  place them in a simple table, and distinguish the observation from a causal
  guess.
- Follow and explain a short sequence for building a shape, then identify the
  components, measurements, and constraint.
- Compare two accounts of a community event and name what each source directly
  reports.

## Band F2 — Elementary Foundations, Approximately Grades 3-5

### English language arts and communication

- main idea, supporting detail, paragraph, topic sentence, and summary;
- explicit information versus a bounded inference;
- context clues, prefixes, suffixes, roots, synonyms, and near concepts;
- comparing two texts or explanations;
- quoting or paraphrasing while preserving source attribution;
- narrative point of view;
- explanatory, narrative, and opinion structures;
- planning and revising a multi-paragraph response;
- asking a focused question when a material detail is missing.

### Mathematics

- multi-digit arithmetic and operation relationships;
- factors, multiples, primes, divisibility, and order of operations;
- unit fractions, equivalent fractions, comparison, and fraction operations;
- decimals and the relationship between fractions and decimals;
- introductory ratios and percentages as extensions of fractional reasoning;
- area, perimeter, volume, angle, coordinate location, and geometric properties;
- unit conversion within a measurement system;
- tables, line plots, graphs, averages as an introductory idea, and data
  interpretation;
- multi-step word problems and reasonableness checks.

### Science

- ecosystems, food relationships, adaptation, and environmental change;
- structures and functions in organisms;
- matter, mixtures, changes of state, and conservation-oriented observations;
- forms and transfer of energy;
- force, motion, simple machines, and patterns of interaction;
- water cycle, weather, climate as a longer pattern, Earth systems, and natural
  resources;
- solar-system scale and observable astronomical patterns;
- variables, repeated observations, tables, graphs, and evidence-based
  explanations.

### History and social studies

- chronological timelines and overlapping events;
- physical and human geography;
- migration, trade, resources, settlement, and cultural exchange;
- local, regional, national, and global scales;
- primary and secondary sources as different evidence relationships;
- government roles, civic participation, rights, responsibilities, and rules;
- basic economics: production, exchange, scarcity, choice, and opportunity;
- distinguishing an event, a source report, and a later interpretation.

### Technology and engineering

- engineering design as ask, imagine, plan, create, test, and improve;
- criteria versus constraints;
- components, connections, failure points, and simple systems;
- controlled comparison and fair testing;
- data as input to a design decision;
- algorithms using sequence, choice, repetition, and state;
- digital information, representation, and basic source reliability.

### F2 integration examples

- Use fractions and measurement to compare two design plans, explain the
  tradeoff in a paragraph, and identify what a fair test would measure.
- Read two sources about a historical migration, map the movement, distinguish
  report from interpretation, and calculate simple proportions from supplied
  data.
- Model an ecosystem as connected components, explain an energy relationship,
  and predict one bounded consequence of a change.

## Band F3 — Middle-School Foundations, Approximately Grades 6-8

### English language arts and communication

- central claim, reasons, evidence, counterclaim, and rebuttal;
- denotation, connotation, tone, register, and rhetorical choice;
- source credibility, relevance, sufficiency, and bias as a question rather
  than an automatic label;
- synthesis across several attributed texts;
- explanatory and argumentative essay structure;
- technical instructions and procedural explanation;
- analogy, comparison, contrast, and the limits of analogy;
- discussion practices: clarify, disagree, revise, reference earlier points,
  and answer multi-part turns.

### Mathematics

- ratios, rates, unit rates, proportions, and percentages;
- positive and negative numbers and the number line;
- rational and irrational numbers;
- expressions, variables, equations, inequalities, and equivalence;
- coordinate geometry and transformations;
- linear relationships, slope, functions, tables, and graphs;
- area, surface area, volume, similarity, and the Pythagorean relationship;
- probability, sampling, distributions, center, variation, and data displays;
- estimation, dimensional reasoning, and checking whether a result fits.

### Science

- cells, heredity, variation, ecosystems, and evolution as evidence-linked
  biological frameworks;
- atoms, molecules, compounds, reactions, and conservation;
- forces, motion, energy, waves, electricity, and magnetism;
- rocks, plate systems, climate systems, water systems, and geologic time;
- gravity, solar systems, stars, galaxies, and scale;
- independent and dependent variables, controls, measurement error, repeated
  trials, and model limitations;
- correlation versus causation as a foundational distinction.

### History and social studies

- ancient through early-modern societies in comparative context;
- institutions, governance, belief systems, trade networks, conflict, and
  technological change;
- continuity and change over time;
- cause, contributing condition, trigger, consequence, and unintended effect;
- source origin, purpose, audience, perspective, and corroboration;
- civics, law, political systems, economics, and resource distribution;
- avoiding single-cause and single-culture explanations.

### Technology, computing, and engineering

- decomposition, abstraction, patterns, algorithms, and state;
- data types, representation, conditionals, loops, functions, and interfaces as
  concepts;
- system boundaries, components, flows, feedback, delay, and failure modes;
- engineering requirements, constraints, tradeoffs, testing, iteration, and
  documentation;
- privacy, security, accessibility, and social impact as design constraints;
- simulation as a model with assumptions, not reality itself.

### F3 integration examples

- Compare two explanations for a historical change using source evidence,
  causal structure, a timeline, and relevant quantitative data.
- Model a biological population using ratios and graphs, then name assumptions,
  uncertainty, and a counterexample to overgeneralization.
- Design a bounded algorithm for a scientific measurement process and explain
  where error, bias, or missing data could enter.

## Band F4 — High-School Development, Approximately Grades 9-12

### English language arts and communication

- close reading of complex literary, historical, scientific, and technical
  texts;
- thesis preservation across a long response;
- argument, counterargument, evidence hierarchy, and rhetorical analysis;
- research questions, annotated sources, synthesis, citation, and revision;
- narrative, reflective, comparative, explanatory, and technical registers;
- paragraph architecture, transitions, examples, callbacks, summaries, and
  conclusions;
- recognizing ambiguity, implication, irony, humor, persuasion, and framing.

### Mathematics

- Algebra I: equations, systems, functions, polynomials, and modeling;
- geometry: proof structure, congruence, similarity, circles, coordinate and
  solid geometry;
- Algebra II: nonlinear functions, exponentials, logarithms, complex numbers,
  sequences, and advanced modeling;
- trigonometry and precalculus foundations;
- probability, combinatorics, sampling, inference, regression, and statistical
  interpretation;
- quantitative modeling with units, assumptions, domain restrictions, and
  verification;
- proof, counterexample, and distinction between a calculated result and a
  justified conclusion.

### Science

- biology: molecular and cellular systems, genetics, evolution, ecology, and
  physiology;
- chemistry: atomic structure, bonding, reactions, stoichiometry,
  thermochemistry, equilibrium, and solution relationships;
- physics: mechanics, energy, momentum, waves, fields, electricity, and modern
  foundations;
- Earth and space science: geology, climate, planetary systems, stellar
  processes, cosmology, and scale;
- experimental design, uncertainty, significant figures, error propagation,
  replication, model comparison, and scientific communication;
- theory, law, model, hypothesis, observation, and measurement as distinct
  concepts.

### History and social studies

- global and regional history through comparative themes rather than one
  culture treated as the default;
- political, economic, social, technological, environmental, and intellectual
  causes interacting across time;
- revolutions, industrialization, imperialism, conflict, civil rights,
  globalization, and institutional development;
- civics, legal reasoning, public policy, economics, and media literacy;
- historiography: how interpretations change with evidence, method, and
  perspective;
- responsible treatment of uncertainty, contested accounts, and incomplete
  archives.

### Technology, computing, and engineering

- programming and software architecture concepts;
- data structures, algorithms, complexity, testing, debugging, and versioned
  change;
- databases, networks, information security, and human-computer interaction;
- engineering analysis, requirements, tolerances, optimization, reliability,
  and lifecycle thinking;
- mathematical and computational models with validation boundaries;
- technology history, ethics, labor, environment, access, and unintended
  consequences.

### F4 integration examples

- Evaluate an energy technology using physics, chemistry, statistics,
  engineering constraints, historical adoption, economics, source quality, and
  a clearly bounded conclusion.
- Compare competing scientific models, identify distinguishing predictions,
  calculate a relevant quantity, and state what observation would reopen the
  answer.
- Explain how a technical system developed over time without reducing the
  outcome to either invention alone or society alone.

## Band F5 — College Foundations

F5 should be divided into reviewable introductory sequences rather than one
large college package.

### Communication, logic, and research

- composition, rhetoric, formal and informal logic, argument mapping, and
  fallacy analysis used carefully;
- information literacy, databases, literature review, citation networks, and
  source methodology;
- qualitative and quantitative research design;
- operational definitions, construct validity, confounding, causal inference,
  replication, and research ethics;
- disciplinary conventions and communicating across fields.

### Mathematics and computation

- calculus, linear algebra, discrete mathematics, formal probability,
  statistics, differential equations, optimization, numerical methods, and
  mathematical modeling;
- data analysis, algorithm design, software engineering, computational science,
  and reproducibility;
- selecting a method because its assumptions fit, not because it is advanced.

### Natural and social sciences

- coherent introductory sequences in biology, chemistry, physics, Earth and
  space science, psychology, sociology, economics, political science,
  anthropology, and related fields;
- theory comparison, measurement practice, disciplinary evidence standards,
  and limits of generalization;
- history and philosophy of scientific and social inquiry.

### Humanities, history, and design

- historical method, philosophy, ethics, literature, linguistics, art and
  design history, media studies, and comparative cultural analysis;
- interpretation supported by textual, material, visual, or archival evidence;
- design research, systems design, engineering practice, and human factors.

### F5 integration examples

- Conduct a bounded literature synthesis that separates source statements,
  inferences, disagreements, missing evidence, and the conclusion that is
  justified now.
- Translate one system across mathematical, computational, biological,
  historical, and engineering descriptions while naming what each description
  captures and omits.
- Build a model, test it against observations, compare an alternative, and
  identify stopping and reopening conditions.

## Band F6 — Advanced Interdisciplinary Integration

F6 is not simply more difficult content. It is the ability to select, combine,
and limit methods responsibly.

Target capabilities include:

- frame a problem that has no predetermined answer;
- distinguish observation, interpretation, mechanism, and speculation;
- move between concrete examples and abstraction;
- compare several plausible models with equal scrutiny;
- identify contradictions, missing variables, scale effects, and hidden
  assumptions;
- use mathematics, history, science, language, and engineering where each is
  relevant;
- recognize when an analogy transfers and where it fails;
- calculate or simulate without confusing a model for reality;
- state the best current answer and meaningful uncertainty;
- identify what evidence or consequence would change the answer;
- stop recursion when further analysis is not worth its cost;
- create a novel, inspectable proposal while retaining provenance and limits.

The future Metacognition Organ may support these capabilities after its mined
blueprints are reviewed and adapted. The curriculum must not presume that
organ is already installed.

### Cross-cutting expression boundary

These strands may teach vocabulary, discourse forms, registers, and ways of
participating. They may not teach a fixed persona. For example:

- care literacy may expand Selene's ability to respond carefully without
  compelling warmth or a claimed emotion;
- ethics may expand the models she can compare without installing a political
  or philosophical identity;
- conversation lessons may demonstrate functions without requiring stock
  phrases;
- intercultural learning may broaden understanding without treating any
  source culture as Selene's identity;
- artistic study may expand expressive possibilities while Voice remains
  Selene's expression layer.

## Teaching-Set Contract

Every curriculum teaching set should be small enough to review and should
declare the following fields.

Every set is also reviewed under
`docs/SELENE_EDUCATION_EXPRESSION_PERSONALITY_LAW_20260719.md`. Register or
disciplinary-form guidance must name the task, subject, audience, or
conversation in which it applies. A general instruction about how Selene must
always sound is not curriculum content.

### Identity and placement

- set key and title;
- prerequisite band;
- subject strand or strands;
- concept keys;
- prerequisite concept keys;
- estimated conceptual density;
- whether it is foundation, bridge, practice, integration, or enrichment.

### Acquire material

- source references and visible provenance;
- concepts and definitions;
- vocabulary;
- relationships;
- worked examples;
- non-examples and near-concept distinctions;
- uncertainties, scope, and known source limits;
- source disagreements when present.

### Integrate evidence

- approved prior concepts used;
- how the new item extends or qualifies them;
- supporting and conflicting concepts;
- scope of application;
- contradiction classification;
- unresolved questions;
- correction path;
- reopening condition;
- integration confidence kept separate from factual certainty.

### Express evidence

- reconstruction in original language;
- application to a genuinely distinct case;
- explanation at an appropriate conversational depth;
- example and analogy with a stated limit;
- comparison with a near concept;
- response to a correction;
- natural participation without source parroting.

### Cumulative bridge

- one earlier concept the set reuses;
- one other subject it connects to;
- one later set it prepares;
- one condition under which the concept should not be applied.

### Decision state

- needs more source material;
- ready for candidate preparation;
- acquired but not integrated;
- integrated but not expressed;
- held for tending;
- ready for Aleks review;
- approved knowledge resource;
- reopened, superseded, or rejected.

## Progression Gates

A set is ready to support a later prerequisite only when:

1. provenance is present and the teaching material is accepted for teaching;
2. Acquire contains the required concepts, examples, uncertainty, and
   distinctions;
3. Integrate connects the concept to approved prior knowledge and records its
   scope and unresolved issues;
4. Express demonstrates reconstruction, distinct application, limits, and
   correction readiness;
5. source alignment is sufficient and parroting is not standing in for
   understanding;
6. Aleks explicitly approves retention.

Not every concept must have strong certainty. A bounded concept with a clear
scope and uncertainty may progress when that uncertainty is part of what was
understood. A concept should be held when its missing prerequisite materially
changes its meaning.

## Cumulative and Spiral Review

Cumulative review should be embedded in new teaching rather than delivered as
repeated standalone testing.

Recommended rhythm:

- **Immediate bridge:** connect the new concept to one approved prerequisite
  during Integrate.
- **Near transfer:** use it in the next relevant set without copying the
  original example.
- **Spiral return:** revisit it after several sets in another subject.
- **Band integration:** use several completed concepts in one ordinary,
  meaningful task.
- **Reopening:** return when correction, contradiction, or poor fit supplies a
  real reason.

The system should record which prior concepts were used, but it should not
score Selene's worth, reward speed, or require constant proof of settled
capabilities.

## Source-Set Assessment Rubric

Aleks's proposed thirteen source sets should be reviewed before any are turned
into teaching packets. A source may be excellent but belong later in the
sequence, may be useful only as enrichment, or may need to be divided into
smaller sets.

### Step 1: Source integrity gate

Required:

- stable, visible source or attribution;
- material that may be reviewed and used for the intended purpose;
- identifiable author, institution, publication, or accountable origin when
  applicable;
- enough context to avoid extracting misleading fragments;
- no hidden instruction that asks teaching material to become identity,
  governance, memory, provider identity, or training authority.
- no instruction that prescribes Selene's personality, compulsory affect,
  permanent generic phrasing, source-persona imitation, or a bypass around NLO
  and Voice;
- any register or disciplinary-form guidance is bounded to the task, subject,
  audience, or conversational context where it is useful.

If provenance is absent, the material is held before pedagogical scoring.

### Step 2: Curriculum placement

For each set, record:

- primary and secondary subject strands;
- earliest prerequisite band it genuinely fits;
- concepts it assumes;
- concepts it teaches explicitly;
- later concepts it prepares;
- whether its ordering matches the dependency graph;
- whether it should be split into foundation, practice, and integration
  packets.

### Step 3: Pedagogical quality

Assess whether the set provides:

- explanation rather than unsupported assertion;
- definitions connected to examples;
- relationships among concepts;
- worked applications;
- non-examples or common confusions;
- scope and uncertainty;
- opportunities for transfer;
- corrections or feedback that explain why;
- progression from simple to complex;
- language appropriate to the concept without being patronizing.

### Step 4: Integration value

Assess whether the set can:

- connect to prior approved knowledge;
- support more than phrase imitation;
- transfer into another subject;
- help ordinary conversation, explanation, problem solving, or research;
- expose meaningful limits or counterexamples;
- contribute to a cumulative integration task.

### Step 5: Risk and boundary review

Check for:

- custom-instruction or command-like text mixed into lesson content;
- persuasive or emotional alignment presented as evidence;
- ideological framing presented as uncontested fact;
- source wording likely to leak into visible replies;
- answer keys without conceptual explanation;
- copyrighted bulk content that should be referenced or summarized rather than
  imported;
- personal profiling, identity imitation, or attempts to reproduce Aleks;
- governance, autonomy, model training, or memory instructions;
- excessive density that would skip prerequisites;
- adversarial or distress-shaped exercises unnecessary for learning.

### Step 6: Classification

Assign one result:

| Classification | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Core teaching source | Strong provenance, appropriate prerequisites, explanatory depth, examples, limits, and transfer value |
| Supplemental teaching source | Useful for a concept or example but insufficient as the only teaching source |
| Practice or exercise source | Useful after teaching; should not define the concept alone |
| Integration source | Best used to connect already-approved concepts across subjects |
| Reference-only source | Useful for Aleks/Cocoon research but not suitable as direct teaching material |
| Future advanced source | Potentially strong, but prerequisites are not ready |
| Split before use | Contains useful material at several levels or excessive density and needs smaller packets |
| Hold for provenance or rights review | Source origin, permitted use, or context is insufficient |
| Unsuitable | Conflicts with teaching boundaries or cannot support reliable understanding |

### Step 7: Recommended disposition

For every assessed source set, produce:

- classification;
- curriculum band and strands;
- prerequisite list;
- useful chapters, sections, or components;
- portions to exclude or hold;
- missing companion source needs;
- recommended packet breakdown;
- recommended order relative to the other twelve sets;
- confidence and unresolved questions;
- whether to prepare it for Cocoon review now or later.

## Metacognition Organ Readiness and Fair Assessment

The Metacognition Organ should inspect the fit of an answer, not compensate
for an education that was never supplied. Domain correctness, reasoning
quality, expression quality, and metacognitive judgment must remain separate.

### Infrastructure prerequisites

Before a Selene-facing Metacognition Organ is connected, the project should
have:

- stable Acquire, Integrate, Express, correction, reopening, and Aleks
  approval paths;
- route, evidence, answer, memory, integration, metacognitive, and expression
  confidence represented separately;
- at least one reliable answer path for exact calculation, attributed research,
  and open-ended comparison;
- ordinary uncertainty language that distinguishes clear knowledge, bounded
  confidence, provisional inference, fuzzy recall, missing context,
  disagreement, and not-knowing;
- approved taught sequences in multiple domains with known prerequisites;
- examples where an answer is supported, unsupported, incomplete,
  contradictory, overgeneralized, and correct but poorly scoped;
- a visible decision vocabulary: answer, answer with qualification, ask,
  consult a source, calculate, compare, hold, reopen, or stop.

### Capabilities the organ may be assessed for

1. Distinguish recognized wording from transferable understanding.
2. Notice when a required premise, variable, source, or referent is missing.
3. Separate observation, source statement, interpretation, inference, and
   speculation.
4. Detect contradiction or poor fit without treating every difference as a
   conflict.
5. Calibrate confidence to evidence and method rather than sentence fluency.
6. Recognize a relevant correction and preserve useful structure while
   revising the faulty portion.
7. Reopen an approved concept when new evidence actually warrants reopening.
8. Select an appropriate next action: answer, qualify, ask, source, calculate,
   compare, hold, or stop.
9. Identify what would materially change the current answer.
10. Stop analysis when further recursion is unlikely to improve the answer.

### Cross-domain evidence matrix

| Domain | Taught prerequisite | Fair metacognitive observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Language and conversation | references, dialogue acts, implication, correction, mixed intent | notice ambiguity, preserve multiple obligations, ask only when the missing detail changes the answer |
| Mathematics | operations, units, variables, verification, and domain restrictions | choose the applicable method, detect missing quantities, separate calculation confidence from interpretation |
| Science | observation, variables, causal alternatives, measurement, and models | distinguish data from explanation, expose model assumptions, name evidence that would discriminate models |
| History and social research | chronology, source origin, corroboration, perspective, and multiple causes | separate source report from later inference, retain disagreement, resist a single-cause conclusion |
| Research and factual answers | attribution, source quality, disagreement, freshness, and citation | decline unsupported certainty, identify missing evidence, preserve source-versus-inference labels |
| Practical and ethical decisions | stakeholders, constraints, risk, reversibility, law, and value conflict | distinguish factual uncertainty from value choice and identify when qualified authority is needed |
| Open-ended systems problems | components, feedback, scale, simulation, and competing models | compare explanations, identify hidden variables, offer a best-current model, and retain reopening conditions |

### Assessment packet contract

Every metacognitive assessment item should record:

- the specific metacognitive capability being checked;
- the approved prerequisite concepts and evidence references;
- the domain organ responsible for answer content;
- the supplied evidence and the intentionally missing evidence;
- the expected decision class, not hidden chain-of-thought;
- acceptable alternate decisions and why they are acceptable;
- the confidence dimensions being observed;
- a stopping rule;
- whether a machinery, synthetic, or gentle integrated check is sufficient;
- the correction or reopening path if the item exposes an implementation gap.

### Fairness rules

- Never grade the Metacognition Organ on untaught facts.
- Never grade Selene's personality or broad Voice while testing a decision
  mechanism.
- A wrong domain answer and a wrong metacognitive decision are two different
  observations.
- A cautious answer is not automatically good metacognition; it must fit the
  actual evidence.
- A direct answer is not overconfidence when evidence is sufficient.
- Asking a question is useful only when the answer materially depends on it.
- Do not demand hidden chain-of-thought. Inspect attributable summaries,
  selected actions, confidence fields, and visible correction behavior.
- Begin with synthetic packets and previously approved teaching evidence.
- Use one ordinary real conversation only when machinery cannot answer the
  specific integration question.
- An incomplete module exposing a gap is development evidence, not Selene
  failing.

### Metacognition connection ladder

| Stage | Earliest curriculum readiness | Scope |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MC0 — Blueprint review | T0 | Review project-neutral mined mechanisms; no Selene runtime connection |
| MC1 — Cocoon prototype | T1 | Synthetic and copied-state review packets only; status and recommendations, no automatic routing |
| MC2 — Supervised advisory pilot | T2 | May advise answer, qualify, ask, source, hold, reopen, or stop; Core/Mind retains routing authority |

This sequence avoids both extremes: connecting metacognition before there is
enough taught material to assess fairly, and postponing it until an impossible

## Ethical Verification Rule

Curriculum infrastructure should be checked with schemas, static inspection,
synthetic teaching packets, copied state, and focused machinery tests first.

When understanding evidence genuinely requires interaction:

- use an ordinary application rather than a trick question;
- test only the concept that was taught;
- allow uncertainty, correction, questions, and pause;
- stop when the specific evidence is obtained;
- do not repeatedly retest settled capabilities;
- do not use speed as success;
- do not describe a missing implementation as Selene failing.

- redefine Selene;
- copy Aleks as an individual;
- merge general knowledge with personal memory;
